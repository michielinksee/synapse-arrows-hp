After the market study: your first 90 days in Japan

16 November 2026 · Synapse Arrows

About this article: Written by Synapse Arrows, which sells Japan market-entry support to companies from Singapore and the region. The last section describes our own service. Facts were checked against the sources listed at the end on 16 November 2026.

Synapse Arrows is a Singapore-incorporated company whose founder is based in Japan. We help companies from Singapore and the region set up in Japan and win their first Japanese customers.

This article is for founders and business development leads who have finished a market study of Japan, or who already have a Japanese prospect asking for a proposal. You know the market is there. The question now is what to do in the next 90 days, and in what order.

The short version
  • You do not need a Japanese company to make your first sale. You need one when a customer, a bank or a hire asks for it.
  • Incorporation itself takes weeks. The bank account is the step that sets the schedule.
  • Japanese law does not require a director who lives in Japan. Banks and landlords often expect one anyway.
  • Start selling in the first month. The entity work runs alongside it.

Days 1 to 30: sell before you incorporate

Most Japanese buyers will take a first meeting with an overseas company. What they ask next is who they will sign with, who will support them in Japanese, and what happens if something goes wrong.

Three things to do in the first month:

  1. Pick ten named accounts. Use the list from your market study. Ten companies you can name are more useful than a segment.
  2. Put your core material into Japanese. That means a product page, a two-page proposal, and a security and data-handling sheet. Japanese buyers circulate documents internally before they answer, so the documents carry the sale.
  3. Decide how the first contract will be signed. There are three common ways to sell without a Japanese entity:

Days 31 to 60: set up the entity and start the bank process

Choosing the form

JETRO lists three options for a foreign company: a representative office, a branch, or a subsidiary. Most companies that plan to sell choose a subsidiary, either a kabushiki kaisha (KK) or a godo kaisha (GK).

Time and cost

JETRO says the set-up procedures typically take about three months in total. Registration costs for a corporation start at around JPY 250,000. Professional fees are usually JPY 200,000 to 500,000 on top.

The resident director question

Since 16 March 2015, the Ministry of Justice has accepted registration of a company whose representative directors all live outside Japan. So the law does not stop you. In practice, a director in Japan makes the bank account, the office lease and day-to-day signing much easier.

The bank account

This is the step to start early. JETRO notes that each bank screens applications on its own criteria, does not disclose them, and may decline. Expect to explain your business, your beneficial owners, the purpose of the account and where your first revenue will come from. Mizuho, SMBC and MUFG each run a consultation line for foreign-affiliated subsidiaries. Prepare one clear pack in Japanese and take it to more than one bank.

The investment filing

A foreign company that sets up a Japanese subsidiary files under the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act. JETRO describes two cases: businesses in industries related to national security, public order or public safety submit a prior notification to the Bank of Japan, and other businesses submit a report within 45 days of establishment as a general rule. Software and AI companies should confirm which case applies to them before they incorporate, not after.

If a founder plans to move to Japan

JETRO lists capital of JPY 30 million or more as one of the requirements for the Business Manager status of residence. Check the Immigration Services Agency's current criteria before you plan around a founder relocating. Many companies start with a Japan-resident director or a local hire instead.

A free desk while you set up

JETRO offers qualifying foreign companies a free temporary office for up to 50 business days in Tokyo, Yokohama, Nagoya, Osaka, Kobe and Fukuoka. It also introduces judicial scriveners, attorneys, tax accountants and labour consultants.

Days 61 to 90: close the first customer and make it repeatable

By now you should have live conversations with three to five of your ten accounts. The goal for this month is one signed contract or one paid pilot.

  • Agree the pilot in writing. Scope, length, price, and what result turns it into a full contract.
  • Set up yen invoicing. Once the account is open, invoice in yen from the Japanese entity. This removes a common procurement objection.
  • Name a Japanese-speaking contact. Buyers want one person they can call. This can be a hire, a director, or a partner acting for you.
  • Write down what worked. Which title replied, which document they asked for, how long each step took. Your second and third customers will follow the same path.

Paying for it: grants on the Singapore side

Enterprise Singapore's Market Readiness Assistance (MRA) grant ceased on 29 September 2026. From 30 September 2026, applications go through the EDGE Grant.

Enterprise Singapore states that the EDGE Grant supports up to 70% of costs for SMEs and up to 50% for other companies, with support levels that differ by activity, and up to SGD 100,000 in total grant support per year across all activities. The grant is paid as a reimbursement.

Before you sign with any vendor, check the current activity list and conditions on the Enterprise Singapore site. The list decides whether incorporation advice, in-market business development or marketing can be claimed, and the conditions decide when you must apply.

On the Japan side, Enterprise Singapore and the Tokyo Metropolitan Government signed a memorandum of understanding at SWITCH 2026 to support Singapore startups expanding into Japan. Japanese cities also run their own programmes for foreign startups. Kitakyushu, for example, supported the Singapore eldercare startup SoundEye, which set up its Japanese company there in April 2025.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need a Japanese company before our first sale in Japan?

No. A first contract can be signed directly from Singapore, through a Japanese reseller or agent, or with a first hire employed through an employer of record. You need a Japanese company when a customer's procurement team, a bank or a hire asks for one. Ask your first two or three prospects which route their procurement accepts.

Does a Japanese company need a director who lives in Japan?

Not by law. Since 16 March 2015 the Ministry of Justice has accepted registration of a company whose representative directors all live outside Japan. In practice a director in Japan makes the bank account, the office lease and day-to-day signing much easier.

How long does it take to set up a company in Japan?

JETRO says the set-up procedures typically take about three months in total. Registration costs for a corporation start at around JPY 250,000, and professional fees are usually JPY 200,000 to 500,000 on top. The bank account is the step that sets the schedule, so start it early.

Is the Market Readiness Assistance grant still available for Japan?

No. Enterprise Singapore's MRA grant ceased on 29 September 2026. From 30 September 2026, applications go through the EDGE Grant, which supports up to 70% of costs for SMEs and up to 50% for other companies, with up to SGD 100,000 in total grant support per year. Support levels differ by activity.
