Selling in Japan without a Japanese company: how far can you go?

16 November 2026 · Synapse Arrows

About this article: Written by Synapse Arrows, which sells Japan market-entry support to companies from Singapore and the region. The last section describes our own service. The rules below come from the official pages listed at the end, read on 16 November 2026. This is general information, not legal or tax advice.

Synapse Arrows is a Singapore-incorporated company whose founder is based in Japan. We help companies from Singapore and the region set up in Japan and work towards their first Japanese customers.

This article is for founders who have a Japanese prospect and no Japanese company. It sets out what each way of selling allows, what to check on registration, tax and customs, and the signs that it is time to register.

The short version
  • A cross-border sale to a Japanese customer does not in itself require a Japanese company.
  • A foreign company that wishes to engage in continuous transactions in Japan must register in Japan.
  • A representative office cannot sell. A distributor sells in its own name. An agent introduces the sale and you remain the seller.
  • Registration, corporate tax, consumption tax and customs are four separate questions. Clearing one does not clear the others.

The rule that sets the limit

JETRO states that foreign companies wishing to engage in continuous transactions in Japan must register in the country, citing Article 818 of the Companies Act. They must register at least one of four things: the appointment of a representative in Japan, the establishment of a branch office, a Japanese corporation, or a partnership.

Article 818 also provides that a person who carries out transactions in breach of this rule is liable, jointly with the foreign company, for the obligations that arise from them. The Ministry of Justice notes that a civil fine may apply as well.

The official pages we read do not define where a series of sales becomes continuous transactions. That is the question to put to a lawyer in Japan, with your actual contracts and plans in hand.

Direct from your home company

Your company signs with the Japanese customer and delivers from abroad. This is the simplest start. Its limits are practical: the customer contracts with an overseas company and pays across the border, and has no one to call in Japan. It is also the route where the continuous-transactions question arrives first as sales repeat.

A Japanese distributor

JETRO describes a distributor as a party to the sale. It buys outright, sets its own prices, and bears the profit, the loss and the collection risk. A distribution agreement may limit the products, set minimum sales, or require inventory, parts and after-sales service. The end customer gets a domestic contract with the distributor.

A Japanese agent

JETRO describes an agent as someone who does not become a party to the sales contract. Your company is the seller, bears all the risk including non-payment, and pays the agent a commission. JETRO warns against confusing the two terms, because a contract labelled as agency leaves the receivables risk with you.

An agent can also matter for tax. See the section on permanent establishment below.

A representative office

JETRO states that a representative office may conduct market surveys, collect information, purchase goods and carry out publicity and advertising, and that it is not permitted to engage in sales activities. It does not require registration. It cannot ordinarily open a bank account or lease real estate in its own name; the head office or the representative as an individual signs those agreements.

Four separate questions to check

1. Registration

This is the Companies Act question above: are you engaging in continuous transactions in Japan? If so, JETRO names the branch office as the simplest means of setting up a base, and a subsidiary as the other common choice.

2. Corporate tax and permanent establishment

How a foreign corporation's Japan business is taxed depends on whether it has a permanent establishment (PE) in Japan. JETRO's definition covers a fixed place of business such as a branch, office or factory, a long-term construction site, and an agent for the conclusion of contracts. A fixed place used solely for purchasing or collecting information, or solely for storage, display and delivery, is outside the definition where its function is preliminary or ancillary.

So a person in Japan who concludes contracts for you can change your tax position, whatever the answer on registration. A tax treaty between Japan and your home country may also change the result. Take this to a tax adviser before you give anyone in Japan authority to sign.

3. Consumption tax

Japan's consumption tax is 10% in principle. For companies that sell software or online services from abroad, JETRO sets out three rules:

  • Sales to businesses. For certain cross-border electronic services, the Japanese business that receives the service is responsible for filing and payment. This is the reverse-charge rule.
  • Sales to consumers. For certain cross-border electronic services to consumers, the foreign supplier is responsible for filing and payment.
  • Sales through platforms. For consumer services sold through certain digital platforms on or after 1 April 2025, certain platform operators file and pay.

Business customers may also ask whether you issue qualified invoices. JETRO explains that since 1 October 2023 a buyer cannot fully credit the tax on purchases from a seller that is not a qualified invoice issuer. A transitional measure allows 50% from 1 October 2026 to 30 September 2029. A foreign corporation that needs to file tax returns in Japan appoints a tax agent and submits a notification.

4. Customs, if you ship goods

Japan Customs states that a person living abroad designates a person in Japan as a Customs Procedure Agent, who must have an address or residence in Japan. An agent who is not a licensed customs broker may not file import declarations as a business, so a licensed customs broker handles those. Check product-specific rules for your goods separately.

One more point on people. Engaging a person in Japan through an employer of record does not by itself answer the registration or PE questions. What that person does for you is what matters.

Signs that it is time to register

  • Sales to Japanese customers have become regular, or you have signed a contract that runs over time.
  • Someone in Japan negotiates and concludes contracts on your behalf.
  • A customer's procurement team asks for a domestic contracting party and you do not want a distributor between you.
  • You need a bank account or an office lease in the company's name.
  • You are ready to hire in Japan.

When you reach that point, see your first 90 days in Japan for the set-up steps, whether you need a director who lives in Japan, and grants and public support for the cost.

Frequently asked questions

Can a foreign company sell to Japanese customers without a Japanese company?

A cross-border sale to a Japanese customer does not in itself require a Japanese company. JETRO states that a foreign company wishing to engage in continuous transactions in Japan must register in Japan under Article 818 of the Companies Act. Where that line falls for your business is a question for a lawyer in Japan.

Can a representative office sell in Japan?

No. JETRO states that a representative office may conduct market surveys, collect information, purchase goods and carry out publicity and advertising, but is not permitted to engage in sales activities. It does not require registration, and it cannot ordinarily open a bank account or lease real estate in its own name.

What is the difference between an agent and a distributor in Japan?

JETRO explains that an agent does not become a party to the sales contract: the foreign company sells to the customer, bears the risk including non-payment, and pays the agent a commission. A distributor buys the goods and resells them in its own name, sets its own prices and bears the collection risk.

Does Japanese consumption tax apply to a foreign company selling software or online services?

It can. JETRO explains that for certain cross-border electronic services supplied to businesses, the Japanese customer files and pays under a reverse-charge rule, and for those supplied to consumers, the foreign supplier is responsible for filing and payment. Since 1 April 2025, certain platform operators file and pay for consumer services sold through their platforms. The standard rate is 10%.
